# 01 - Assessment Cues

## The idea in one sentence

You can't diagnose what you didn't notice, so step one is noticing — and writing down what you noticed in neutral, specific language.

## Objective data vs. subjective data

- **Objective** = things you can observe or measure: vital signs, wound measurements, lab values, "patient guarding abdomen when repositioning," "bag is not half full by end of shift."
- **Subjective** = things the patient tells you: "it hurts when I walk," "I haven't slept well since admission," "I don't feel like eating."

Both are cues. Neither is "better." A care plan built on only one type is lopsided.

## What makes a good cue

A good cue is:

- **Specific.** "Pain 7/10 when ambulating" beats "pain."
- **Measurable or verifiable.** Someone else reading your note could confirm it.
- **Neutral.** Describe behavior, not judgment. "Patient refused breakfast and lunch, states 'not hungry'" rather than "patient is being difficult."
- **Relevant.** Connected to an actual nursing problem — not just trivia you collected.

## Signs you're collecting bad cues

- It's an inference dressed as a fact: "patient is anxious" (that's a conclusion — the cue is "patient paces, asks three times when surgery is, voice tense, states 'I can't stop worrying'").
- It's vague: "poor intake," "unstable," "deteriorating."
- It's a medical diagnosis with a costume on: a cue list shouldn't be "CHF, COPD, sepsis." Those belong in the patient's *history*; your *diagnosis* comes from the cues' consequences (breath sounds, edema, SpO2, activity tolerance).
- It's a task list: "took vitals, charted I&O, gave PRN." Those are actions, not cues.
- It's scribble you can't reconstruct: "planter on which foot" might mean something to you today, but a stranger reading your chart tomorrow can't use it.

## Practical method

1. **Gather systematically** — head-to-toe or by system, plus the psychosocial side. Whatever framework your program uses; the point is *consistency*, so nothing gets skipped.
2. **Cluster the cues** — group related ones together. Clustering is where the "aha" happens: scattered findings become a pattern, and the pattern points at a diagnosis.
3. **Note what's abnormal *and* what matters** — "planter on which foot"? No — write findings a stranger could reconstruct.

## Mini practice

Turn each raw note into a proper cue:

| Raw note | Better cue |
|---|---|
| "SOB" | "Patient reports shortness of breath after walking 20 feet; respiratory rate 26, SpO2 91% on room air" |
| "not eating much" | "Consumed <25% of each meal over past two days; states food 'tastes like cardboard'" |
| "nice lady but confused sometimes" | "Oriented to person and place, not to date; asks same question repeatedly (every 10–15 min) per staff report" |

Do not paste real patient details into practice work. Use program-provided case studies.
